\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

Imagine an enterprise that has decided to move its systems to a European provider.
It draws up a shortlist, and every provider on it calls itself sovereign. Each can
point to something that seems to prove it: a data centre in Germany, a company
registered in France, a certificate on its website. \Cref{ch:foundations} showed
why none of these settles the matter, which leaves the enterprise with the harder
task of choosing.

Picking the fastest provider would not help, because speed says nothing about who
can reach the data. Picking the one with the most certificates would not help
either, because a certificate shows that a process was followed, not that a foreign
government can be kept out. What the enterprise really needs to know comes down to
four things: whether the provider can lawfully protect the data it holds, whether
it can do the work the enterprise needs done, whether it will still be in business
when the contract ends, and whether it is fast enough at a price worth paying.

Each of these can rule a provider out on its own, and none makes up for another. A
fast provider that can be ordered to hand data over will still have to hand it
over, and a capable provider that runs out of money is still gone. For that reason
this thesis looks at the four separately, and brings them together only at the end,
so that the trade-offs between them stay visible.

\section{Research Design}
\label{sec:research-design}

Each of the four questions became a study of its own, carried out on the same
providers and to the same standards, so that the results could be laid side by
side. \Cref{tab:study-overview} shows the four studies, the question each one
answers and what it set out to find.

           carried out to establish.}

  \small
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l l X X@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{\#} & \textbf{Study} & \textbf{Question} & \textbf{Goal} \\
    \midrule

        \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}

The four studies do not rest on data of the same kind. Only the speed tests were
measured directly, apart from three network results taken from a published source.
The sovereignty and capability studies rely on what providers and regulators have
published, and the financial study on the accounts that companies report. These are
not equally solid, and \cref{sec:evidence-standards} explains how each kind is
marked so that the reader can always tell which is which.

\acs{AWS} is the yardstick throughout. It is not scored for sovereignty, but it
takes part in the other three studies. In the capability study it plays a special
role: the list of services comes from \acs{AWS} itself, so \acs{AWS} scores full
marks by design, and each European provider is measured by how much of that list it
can match. A service that a European provider offers but \acs{AWS} does not earns
no credit, a limit discussed in \cref{sec:design-limitations}.

What is being judged in every study is the provider as a whole, not a single
service or machine. Where one company runs more than one platform, each platform is
judged on its own, and any difference between them is reported. A gap between two
platforms with the same owner is a finding in its own right.

\section{The CloudSov Evaluation Environment}
\label{sec:cloudsov-tool}

Behind the four studies sit a great many separate decisions: a verdict on
every sovereignty question for every provider, and a check of every service against
what Alps Alpine uses, each tied to its own source. Kept in spreadsheets, that work
would be hard to check and almost impossible for anyone else to repeat. It was
therefore done in CloudSov, a small open-source tool built for this thesis, which
stores the data, applies the scoring rules and shows the results side by side.

\begin{quote}
\textit{Repository:} \texttt{https://github.com/aaeu-ihono/cloudsov-tool.git}\\
\textit{Licence:} MIT \\
\textit{Online Access:}  \texttt{https://cloudsov.ihonosetale.eu}\\
\end{quote}

Every decision in CloudSov is stored together with the source it came from, and
every score and chart is worked out from those stored decisions. The weightings and
pass marks are settings rather than fixed rules, so the same data can be scored
again under different assumptions. CloudSov has one section for each study:
sovereignty, readiness, finances and technical benchmarking. \Cref{app:cloudsov} shows these
sections and explains how to run CloudSov on a computer.

Because every provider passes through the same rules, none of them is judged on a
different basis from the others, and anyone with the code and the data can repeat
the calculations and arrive at the same results. Providers, questions and weightings
can all be changed without touching the tool itself, so each study can be extended
or repeated as the market changes.

Two of the eighteen entries are the same platform under two names, since Deutsche
Telekom renamed Open Telekom Cloud as T-Cloud Public \cite{TCloudPublic_2026}. The
sovereignty study looks at both names separately, while the other studies treat
them as one provider. The difference between the two is discussed in
\cref{sec:study1}.

The last two studies cover fewer providers because each study narrows the field for
the next. All eighteen went through the first two studies, and only those that did
well in both, meeting the sovereignty requirements and offering a list of services
close to that of \acs{AWS}, were carried forward. The others were not followed
further, because a provider that cannot lawfully hold the data, or cannot do the
work, does not become a good choice by being cheap or fast.

\section{Data Sources and Collection}
\label{sec:evidence-standards}

The four studies draw on three different kinds of information. The sovereignty and
capability studies rely on what providers publish about themselves: their service
descriptions, their certificates and their company filings. The financial study
relies on the figures that companies report about their own finances. The
benchmarking study relies on measurements, numbers obtained by actually running a
test.

These kinds of information are not equally strong. A document tells the reader what
a company says about itself, while a measurement shows what happened when the
machine was actually run. To keep the difference visible, every answer in the
sovereignty study is stored with a link to the document it came from, and every
source used in the thesis is recorded and cited.

\section{Limitations of these Research Studies}
\label{sec:design-limitations}

Every study in this chapter had to work with what could be found, read or measured
from the outside. That brought limits of its own, and some of them were a real
struggle throughout the research.

\subsection*{Relying on what the providers publish}

The sovereignty and capability studies could only be as good as the documents
behind them. Most providers do not explain in one place who owns them, which laws
apply to them or exactly which services they offer. That information had to be
pieced together from websites, product pages, certificates and company filings,
often written to sell rather than to inform. Where a provider published little,
there was little to judge, and its score suffered for it. A low score in these
studies can therefore mean that a provider explains itself poorly, not necessarily
that it protects data poorly. It also means that the studies record what providers
say about themselves. Whether they act that way in practice could not be tested from
the outside.

\subsection*{Missing financial figures}

The financial study met the same problem in a sharper form. Several of the cloud
businesses studied belong to much larger groups, and those groups report their
results as a whole, without saying how much their cloud business earns on its own.
For T-Cloud Public, STACKIT and Scaleway, the closest published figure had to stand
in for the cloud business, and for Scaleway the last such figure is from 2022
(\cref{tab:study3-growth}). Comparisons with \acs{AWS} for these providers can
therefore only be given as a range, not as a single number. Announced investments
raised a similar difficulty. A company can announce that it will invest a sum by a
certain year, but whether the money is actually spent cannot be seen from outside,
so the study could only record what was promised and by when.

\subsection*{A single moment in time}

Cloud providers change quickly. During this research alone, Deutsche Telekom renamed
its public cloud \cite{TCloudPublic_2026}. Every result in this chapter describes the
providers as they were when the information was collected. A provider that scored
poorly may since have closed some of its gaps, and the opposite is also possible.
The results are a snapshot, not a permanent verdict, which is one reason CloudSov
was built so that the studies can be repeated.

\subsection*{Measuring against \acs{AWS}}

The capability study measures each European provider against the services that
\acs{AWS} offers. This keeps the comparison clear, because it asks how much of what
an enterprise already uses could be replaced. It also has a cost: when a provider
offers something that \acs{AWS} does not, it gets no credit for it. The study
therefore shows how close each provider comes to \acs{AWS}, not everything each
provider does well.

\subsection*{The needs of one company}

The services behind the capability study are the ones used by one enterprise, Alps
Alpine. Another company, with different systems or in a different industry, would
need a different set of services and could reach a different conclusion about the
same providers. The method works for any company, but the results in this chapter
answer the question for this one.

\subsection*{Limits of the performance tests}

The speed tests were the only part of the research that could be measured directly,
and even they had limits. Each provider was tested with one comparable machine, in
one location, over a short period rather than weeks or months. Each test was run
three times, which shows roughly how steady a result is but is too few to prove
that small differences between providers are real (\cref{sec:bench-findings}). For
three of the network results, figures published by Cloud Mercato were used instead,
and these rest on a different number of runs.

\subsection*{What was not assessed}

Some things that matter a great deal to a customer lay outside what this research
could measure. How quickly a provider responds when something goes wrong, how often
its services fail, how good its support is and how easy it is to leave all depend
on experience over time, from inside a working relationship. None of these could be
judged fairly from published material or from a short test, so none of them is part
of the results.
