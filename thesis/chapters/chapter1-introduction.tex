\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:motivation}

Alps Alpine, like many European manufacturers, runs much of its digital work on
\acs{AWS}, the cloud service of the American company Amazon. Servers, storage,
databases and a growing number of artificial intelligence services are rented there
rather than run in the company's own buildings. The arrangement works well: the
services are broad, reliable and quick to set up. What it does not offer is control
over who, in the end, can reach the data.

That question became pressing in the Local Products Department, which was looking
for alternatives to public \acs{AI} services. Artificial intelligence tools learn
from and work on a company's own information, often its most valuable kind, and the
department needed to know where that information would go and under whose rules it
would be handled. Moving such work to a provider based in Europe seemed the obvious
answer, but it raised a set of practical doubts at once. Would a European provider
offer the services the department already relies on? Would it be fast enough, and
affordable? And would it still be in business when a contract of several years came
to an end?

Behind the business problem lies a legal one. American law allows the American
government to demand data from American companies even when that data is stored in
Europe, as \cref{subsec:location-jurisdiction} shows through a case involving
Microsoft. European law, in turn, limits when personal data may leave the Union at
all, and the European Court of Justice has twice cancelled agreements that were meant
to make transfers to the United States safe (\cref{subsec:gdpr-schrems}). Events in
2026 added a further concern: a dispute between the American government and the
\acs{AI} company Anthropic showed that the terms on which an \acs{AI} service is
offered can change because of a conflict in which its customers have no say
(\cref{subsec:ai-providers}). A data centre in Frankfurt does not settle any of this
on its own.

The European Union has answered with a growing body of rules and with a framework of
its own, the Cloud Sovereignty Framework, which sets out what a sovereign cloud
service should provide \cite{EC_DIGIT_CloudSovereignty_2025}. What is missing is a
practical answer for an enterprise: which European providers actually meet those
requirements, what they can offer in place of \acs{AWS}, and at what cost in
services, money and speed. This thesis sets out to provide that answer for Alps
Alpine.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

Three questions guide the thesis. The first asks whether European providers meet the
European Union's own standard for sovereignty, the second how close they come to
\acs{AWS} in what they can offer, and the third what an enterprise gives up on one
side when it gains on the other. Each is answered by the studies in
\cref{ch:evaluation} and brought together in \cref{sec:rq-responses}.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] Can EU cloud providers satisfy the requirements of the EC DG DIGIT
        Cloud Sovereignty Framework?
  \item[RQ2] How does the readiness of EU providers compare to AWS?
  \item[RQ3] What is the trade-off between sovereignty and readiness?
\end{description}

\section{Scope and Delimitation}
\label{sec:scope}

The thesis looks at eighteen European providers, chosen as described in
\cref{ch:landscape}, and compares them with \acs{AWS}. Their sovereignty and their
range of services are judged from what they publish, their finances from what they
and their owners report, and their speed from tests on one size of machine, two
virtual processors with eight gigabytes of memory, in one location per provider.

Some limits follow from this. The results describe the market at one moment, in 2026,
and providers change quickly. The list of services comes from the needs of one
company, Alps Alpine, so another company could reach a different conclusion about the
same providers. How well a provider supports its customers, how often its services
fail and how easy it is to leave could not be judged from the outside, and commercial
terms such as discounts and the cost of moving data out were not compared. These
limits are discussed in full in \cref{sec:design-limitations}. Building and running a
sovereign cloud inside the company was considered and left for future work
(\cref{sec:future-work}).

\section{Structure of this Thesis}
\label{sec:structure}

\Cref{ch:foundations} explains what sovereignty means, why the location of a data
centre is not enough, and how the laws of Europe, the United States and China bear on
cloud services. \Cref{ch:landscape} introduces the eighteen providers and how they were
chosen. \Cref{ch:methodology} describes the four studies, of sovereignty, services,
finances and technical benchmarking, and the CloudSov tool built to carry them out.
\Cref{ch:evaluation} reads the results of the four studies and brings them together.
\Cref{ch:conclusion} answers the research questions and turns the results into
recommendations for European enterprises and for Alps Alpine.
