\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis began with a practical worry at Alps Alpine. Much of the company's
work runs on \acs{AWS}, an American provider, and the company wanted to know
whether it could move that work to a European provider and keep its data under
European control without losing the services it relies on. After four studies of
eighteen providers, the answer is that such a move is possible, but not all at once
and not to a single provider. Some European providers can keep data under European control, others
offer most of the services the company uses, and their machines run as fast as
those of \acs{AWS}, often at a lower price. No provider yet does all of this at the
same time. This chapter answers the three research questions in turn and then turns
the results into advice that an enterprise can act on.

\section{Research Question Responses}
\label{sec:rq-responses}

\subsection{RQ1 --- Sovereignty Framework Compliance}
\label{subsec:rq1}

\begin{quote}
  \itshape Can EU cloud providers satisfy the requirements of the EC DG DIGIT
  Cloud Sovereignty Framework?
\end{quote}

Partly. Some European providers meet the requirements of the European
Commission's framework, but none meets them completely. Five of the eighteen
providers studied, STACKIT, Cyso Cloud, Cleura, Elastx and plusserver, reach every
minimum level set in this thesis (\cref{sec:study1}). Most of the others fall
short in the same two places, the legal protection of data and the origin of the
equipment they run on.

These two shortfalls have very different causes. The legal one is mostly a
problem of the large providers, and it follows them abroad. A provider with
offices, subsidiaries and customers in the United States or Canada can be reached
by those countries' courts and laws, even for data it keeps in Europe. This is a
matter of each provider's own choices, and every provider that falls short could
in principle close the gap itself. The equipment problem is different. No
European provider runs on chips or on the low-level software inside its servers
that were made in Europe, because Europe does not yet make them in the quantities a
cloud needs \cite{EU_ChipsAct_2023}. However well a provider is run, it cannot fix
that alone. European providers can therefore offer a good level of control today,
with European law in force and European staff in charge, but not yet the full
independence the framework describes at its highest level.

\subsection{RQ2 --- Capability Relative to AWS}
\label{subsec:rq2}

\begin{quote}
  \itshape How does the capability of EU providers compare to AWS?
\end{quote}

European providers are well behind \acs{AWS} in the range of services they offer,
but level with it, and often cheaper, when it comes to the speed of a single
machine. No European provider can yet take over everything Alps Alpine runs on
\acs{AWS}. Deutsche Telekom's platform comes closest, offering a little under three
quarters of the services the company uses, while most providers offer around a
quarter (\cref{sec:study2}). The basic building blocks, such as servers and
storage, are widely available. The ready-made services built on top of them, and
above all artificial intelligence, are where Europe falls short.

The picture is brighter when the European providers are seen together rather than
one by one. Between them they already offer about three quarters of what Alps
Alpine uses, so much of the gap could close if each provider built what its
European competitors have already built. On machines of the same size they keep
pace with \acs{AWS} and often cost much less (\cref{sec:study4}). Only the speed at
which data travels between machines is clearly lower. What holds them back is
size. \acs{AWS} earns many times more than any of them and keeps adding new
services every year. At their current pace, T-Cloud Public and STACKIT would offer
what \acs{AWS} offered in 2026 by around 2030 and 2031, but by then \acs{AWS} will
have moved on (\cref{sec:study3}).

\subsection{RQ3 --- The Sovereignty--Capability Trade-off}
\label{subsec:rq3}

\begin{quote}
  \itshape What is the trade-off between sovereignty and capability?
\end{quote}

The more control a European provider gives its customers over their data, the
fewer services it tends to offer. The providers that pass every sovereignty
minimum are mostly small and offer a short list of services, while the platform
with the longest list began as the one with the weakest control, built on
equipment and software from Huawei (\cref{sec:tradeoff}). Today, an enterprise
that wants the most services has to accept less control, and one that wants the
most control has to accept fewer services.

Two things lie behind this. The same missing European chips and server software
that limit how much control any provider can offer also leave artificial
intelligence the weakest part of the European offer. And growing abroad, which
brings a provider more revenue to build new services, also brings it under the
laws of other countries. The trade-off is real, but STACKIT shows that it is not
fixed. It offers the most control of any provider studied, has added services
faster than any of them, and already covers almost half of what Alps Alpine uses.
A provider can grow without giving up control. None has yet grown far enough to
make the choice unnecessary.

\section{Recommendations for EU Enterprises}
\label{sec:recommendations}

No single provider is the right answer for every enterprise, or even for all the
work of one. What fits depends on two things: how sensitive the data is, and what
kind of service the work needs. Putting these two side by side gives the
recommendation matrix in \cref{tab:recommendation-matrix}, which brings together
the results of all four studies.

The top row covers data that is regulated or confidential, where protection from
access outside Europe comes first. Only providers that pass every sovereignty
minimum belong here, and among the providers studied in depth only STACKIT does.
Its machines performed well and its owner stands firmly behind it, but it offers
less than half of the services Alps Alpine uses, so a company that places its
sensitive work there will have to run some services itself. For artificial
intelligence on sensitive data, no European provider can yet be recommended
without conditions. The most promising path is for the company to run openly
available language models itself, on machines rented from a sovereign provider,
but that path still has to be tried in practice.

The middle row covers the everyday internal work of a company, which is private
but not regulated. Here a wide choice of services matters more than passing every
sovereignty test, and T-Cloud Public and OVHcloud offer the widest choice in
Europe. Their weaknesses, business abroad in both cases and the Huawei software
at the heart of T-Cloud Public, matter most for regulated data, which the top row
keeps away from them. For plain servers and storage, OVHcloud gives the best speed
for its price and is the most open about its finances, while IONOS is the
cheapest.

The bottom row covers public or non-sensitive data, and this is where \acs{AWS}
keeps its place. Such work gains little from extra control, and where it depends
on services that no European provider offers, such as a ready-made language-model
service, or on moving large amounts of data between many machines, \acs{AWS}
remains the stronger choice.

Two further points apply to every cell of the matrix. For a long contract,
OVHcloud and STACKIT are the safest choices, for opposite reasons: OVHcloud
publishes its finances in full, while STACKIT has a very strong owner behind it
(\cref{subsec:study3-interpretation}). And because the market is changing
quickly, any choice made today should be looked at again. STACKIT in particular
deserves a fresh look in 2027 and 2028, when its new data-centre campus at
L\"ubbenau starts to open (\cref{sec:study3-investment}) and its list of services
is likely to have grown.

For Alps Alpine, the matrix suggests moving in steps rather than all at once, in
the order marked in \cref{tab:recommendation-matrix}. Confidential data and the
systems that hold it would move first, to STACKIT, starting with the basic servers
and storage where its offer is strongest. Internal work that needs a wider range
of services would follow, to T-Cloud Public or OVHcloud. The artificial
intelligence work that started this search would move last, because this is where
the European offer is weakest, and only once running its own models at a
sovereign provider has proven itself. Moving in this order lets the company take
control of its most sensitive data first, without waiting for Europe to match
\acs{AWS} in everything.

\section{Vendor Lock-in Implications}
\label{sec:lockin}

Leaving \acs{AWS} would gain little if the company simply became tied to a new
provider instead. Here the European market is in good shape. In the sovereignty
study, sixteen of the eighteen providers let customers take their work elsewhere
without being locked in, and the same number use open, publicly described ways of
connecting to their services (\cref{sec:study1}). Many of them are also built on
the same free, openly developed cloud software, called OpenStack. Open Telekom
Cloud was built on it from the start \cite{OTC_GoesLive_2016}, OVHcloud has used it
since 2012 \cite{OVHcloud_OpenStack}, Cyso Cloud describes itself as a European
OpenStack cloud \cite{FugaCloud_About}, and T-Cloud Public names OpenStack among
the open standards it is built on \cite{TCloudPublic_Sovereignty}. Because these
providers share the same underlying software, servers and storage set up at one
can be set up again at another with little change.

The real risk of being tied down lies in the ready-made services that each
provider builds on top, such as its own databases and messaging tools. Each
provider designs these in its own way, so a system built around one has to be
partly rebuilt before it can move. This is also what makes leaving \acs{AWS} hard
today: the more of its ready-made services a company uses, the more it has to
rebuild, and some of them have no European counterpart at all (\cref{sec:study2}).
A company moving to a European provider can avoid repeating the problem by using
open, widely supported tools wherever it can, and by relying on any one provider's
own special services only where it truly needs them.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limits of the four studies were described in \cref{sec:design-limitations}.
They rely on what providers say about themselves, they capture the market at one
moment, they measure every provider against \acs{AWS}, and the services were chosen
for the needs of one company. How well a provider supports its customers, and how
reliable it is over the years, could not be judged from outside. One more limit
applies to the advice in this chapter. Prices were compared using the published
hourly price of one machine size, and the terms that shape a real contract, such
as discounts for long commitments, the cost of taking data out again and the price
of support, were not part of the comparison. In a real purchase these can change
which provider is cheapest, so they should be negotiated before acting on any of
the recommendations above.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The natural next step is to try the advice on a small scale. Moving one real
Alps Alpine system to the recommended provider for its row of the matrix, and
recording over several months what the move involved, what it cost and how well
the service ran, would test under real conditions what this thesis could only
judge from outside, such as support and reliability.

The artificial intelligence work that started this search deserves a trial of its
own. Running openly available language models on machines at a sovereign European
provider, or on the company's own hardware, would show whether this path can
replace public \acs{AI} services for Alps Alpine's needs, and at what cost.

A further study could look at the company building and running its own sovereign
cloud, on free software such as OpenStack. This idea was considered for this
thesis but left out of its scope. In Study~2 the company's own locally hosted
infrastructure offered very few of the services it uses, and a full study would
show what it would take, in equipment, people and money, to change that.

Finally, the assessment should be repeated. The European market is moving fast,
with large public and private investments under way, among them projects
supported through the \acs{IPCEI}-CIS programme \cite{IPCEI_CIS_2023} and
STACKIT's new campus. Because CloudSov keeps the data separate from the rules used
to score it, the four studies can simply be run again on fresh data as these
investments are completed, showing whether the gaps found here are closing.
