\section{Study 2 --- Capability Gap Analysis}
\label{sec:method-study2}

The second study asks a practical question: how much of the work Alps Alpine runs on
\acs{AWS} today could a European provider take over? To answer it, the study takes a
fixed list of sixty \acs{AWS} services and checks, provider by provider, whether
each one offers something equivalent. This section explains where that list came
from and how the answers were turned into scores.

The list is grouped into eleven categories of services, and two further qualities,
how far a provider can grow and how well it performs, are judged directly rather
than counted from the list.

\subsection*{The reference catalogue}

The list starts from the full range of \acs{AWS} services \cite{AWS_Products},
narrowed down to what the company actually uses. Fifty of the sixty services come
from a checklist filled in by Alps Alpine \cite{AlpsAlpine_Checklist}. For each
capability it asked whether it had been needed in the past, is needed now or will
be needed later, and named the \acs{AWS} service that provides it. Fifty-eight lines
were marked as needed, and since several of them name the same product, that leaves
fifty different services. Ten more, widely used elsewhere, were added so that a
provider does not look complete simply because the company happens not to need a
whole category today.

The checklist was filled in against the company's own platform, shown in
\cref{fig:alps-infrastructure} in \cref{app:checklist}, which maps the services the
department runs, is looking into or expects to need. It is laid out in six stages,
from the networking and sign-in services on the left to the applications people use
on the right. The drawing also explains why the list looks the way it does: security
and sign-in account for the largest number of services, while the specialised areas,
machine learning and the management of connected devices, sit furthest from the
foundation.

Because every service on the list is an \acs{AWS} service, \acs{AWS} scores full
marks everywhere by design, and each European provider is measured by how much of
the list it can match. As noted in \cref{sec:research-design}, a provider earns
nothing for a service that \acs{AWS} does not have.

promises and the certificates it holds. \Cref{tab:readiness-dimensions} lists all

\subsection*{The verdicts}

Each service was given one of three verdicts, based on the provider's own published
service descriptions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Full (Y)}: the provider offers a ready-made equivalent that is
        generally available and does much the same job. This is worth two points.
  \item \textbf{Partial (P)}: an equivalent exists but is still in testing, does
        less, or needs a workaround. This is worth one point.
  \item \textbf{None (N)}: there is no ready-made equivalent, so the customer would
        have to build and run it on the provider's basic machines. This is worth
        nothing.
\end{itemize}

A verdict of none does not mean the work cannot be done. It means the provider does
not do it for the customer, so the customer has to do it instead. The gap this study
measures is therefore extra work for the customer rather than a wall it cannot get
past, which is why it is reported separately from the sovereignty results.

\subsection*{From verdicts to a score}

In plain words, a category's score is the points a provider earns on its services,
compared with the points it could have earned, expressed out of 100. Writing $S_c$
for the services in category $c$ and $v_p(s)$ for the points provider $p$ earned on
service $s$:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:readiness-dimension}
  d_{p,c} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{s \in S_c} v_p(s)}{2\,\lvert S_c \rvert}
            \times 100
\end{equation}

Scalability and performance are not worked out this way. Each is given a score
directly on the same scale, with \acs{AWS} at 100.

A provider's overall coverage is the average of all thirteen scores, and its gap is
whatever is left between that average and \acs{AWS}. Because \acs{AWS} scores 100
everywhere, the two always add up to 100:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:readiness-coverage}
  C_p = \frac{1}{13}\sum_{d \in D} d_{p,d}
  \qquad\qquad
  G_p = 100 - C_p
\end{equation}

An example shows how this works. The storage category has five services, so ten
points are possible. STACKIT offers object storage, block storage and managed backup
in full, a cold storage tier that does less than the \acs{AWS} version, and no shared
file system. That gives six points for the three full services, one for the partial
one and none for the last, seven of ten in all, and a storage score of 70.
