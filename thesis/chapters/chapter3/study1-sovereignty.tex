\section{Study 1 --- Sovereignty Scoring}
\label{sec:method-study1}

The first study asks whether each provider can protect the data it holds from
anyone outside Europe. Rather than invent a new way of judging this, the study uses
the Cloud Sovereignty Framework published by the European Commission
\cite{EC_DIGIT_CloudSovereignty_2025}, for the reasons given in
\cref{subsec:definition-adopted}. This section explains how that framework works
and how it was applied to the eighteen providers.

The framework has three parts. It describes five levels of sovereignty, it divides
sovereignty into eight areas, and for each area it lists the things that make a
provider more or less sovereign.

\subsection*{The five assurance levels}

The framework does not treat sovereignty as something a provider either has or
lacks. It measures it in five steps, which it calls Sovereignty Effectiveness
Assurance Levels, or seals for short (\cref{tab:seal-levels}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{The five assurance levels, as defined by the framework
           \cite{EC_DIGIT_CloudSovereignty_2025}.}
  \label{tab:seal-levels}
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Level} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
    \midrule
    SEAL-0 & No sovereignty
      & Service under the exclusive control of non-\acs{EU} parties, governed
        entirely in non-\acs{EU} jurisdictions. \\
    SEAL-1 & Jurisdictional sovereignty
      & \acs{EU} law formally applies but with limited practical
        enforceability; service still under the exclusive control of
        non-\acs{EU} parties. \\
    SEAL-2 & Data sovereignty
      & \acs{EU} law applicable and enforceable, with material non-\acs{EU}
        dependencies remaining; service under their indirect control. \\
    SEAL-3 & Digital resilience
      & \acs{EU} law applicable and enforceable, with \acs{EU} actors
        exercising meaningful but not full influence; service under only
        marginal non-\acs{EU} control. \\
    SEAL-4 & Full digital sovereignty
      & Technology and operations under complete \acs{EU} control, subject only
        to \acs{EU} law, with no critical non-\acs{EU} dependencies. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

All five levels answer the same question: how much control does someone outside the
Union still have? At the first level that control is complete, at the second it is
indirect, at the third it is small, and at the top level it is gone. The scale also
makes a distinction that is easy to miss. European law applying to a service is not
the same as European people being in charge of it, and the levels keep the two
apart.

Two features of this table shape everything that follows. The supply chain carries
the largest weight of the eight, which fits the argument of \cref{sec:full-stack}
that a service can be no more sovereign than the layers beneath it. And the legal
objective has the highest pass mark: a provider must reach the top level there to
pass at all, however well it does elsewhere. The weights come from the framework
itself. The pass marks, the minimum level a provider must reach on each objective,
are left to whoever runs the purchase, and the ones in the last column were set for
this study.

\subsection*{The verdicts}

Each question was given one of three answers:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Yes (Y)}: the provider meets the condition, and a published document
        shows it. This is worth two points.
  \item \textbf{Partly (P)}: the provider meets it only in part, or no published
        document shows that it meets it in full. This is worth one point.
  \item \textbf{No (N)}: the provider does not meet it. This is worth nothing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{From verdicts to a score}

The answers are then turned into a level on each objective and an overall score.
In plain words, the points a provider earns on an objective are compared with the
points it could have earned, which gives a share between nothing and all. Writing
$Q_n$ for the questions of objective $n$ and $v(q)$ for the points an answer is
worth, that share is:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sov-share}
  r_n = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{q \in Q_n} v(q)}{2\,\lvert Q_n \rvert}
\end{equation}

Because every question is worth at most two points, the share always lies between 0
and 1. It is then turned into a level by dividing the range into five equal bands,
each one fifth wide:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sov-seal}
  \text{seal}_n =
  \begin{cases}
    0 & r_n \le 0.20\\
    1 & 0.20 < r_n \le 0.40\\
    2 & 0.40 < r_n \le 0.60\\
    3 & 0.60 < r_n \le 0.80\\
    4 & r_n > 0.80
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The overall score adds the eight levels together, each counted according to its
weight, and expresses the result on a scale from 0 to 100. Dividing a level by four,
the highest level, gives the share of full sovereignty reached on that objective,
and the weight $w_n$ decides how much that objective counts:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sov-score}
  S_{\text{sov}} = \sum_{n=1}^{8} \frac{\text{seal}_n}{4} \times w_n \times 100
\end{equation}

The score is only half of the result. A provider also either passes or fails, and it
passes only if it reaches the pass mark $m_n$ on every one of the eight objectives:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sov-pass}
  \text{pass} \iff \text{seal}_n \ge m_n \quad \text{for every } n = 1,\ldots,8
\end{equation}

An example shows how this works. The legal objective has five questions, so ten
points are possible. A provider with two answers of yes, two of partly and one of no
earns six points, a share of 0.60. That puts it on level~2, which adds five points to
its overall score. But the pass mark on the legal objective is level~4, so the
provider fails.

The score and the pass can therefore tell different stories: a provider can score
well overall and still fail, because one objective falls short. This is
deliberate. It applies within this study the rule set out at the start of
\cref{ch:methodology}, that strength in one place cannot make up for weakness in
another.

The pass marks are settings, not fixed rules. They can be raised or lowered and the
results worked out again, which is how the sensitivity analysis in
\cref{subsec:sensitivity} was carried out.

Reading down the columns shows where the difficulty lies. Almost all the shaded cells
fall in two columns. Ten of the eighteen providers fall short on the legal objective,
where the framework sets the highest pass mark. Ten also fall short on the supply
chain, even though the pass mark there is one of the lowest, and no provider reaches
above level~2 on it at all.

The two shortfalls are different in kind. A provider can improve its legal position
by changing how it is owned or by changing its contracts. It cannot improve its
supply chain in the same way, because the hardware and firmware it runs on are not
made in Europe. This is the problem described in \cref{sec:full-stack} showing up in
the results, and it explains why the objective with the largest weight is also the
one on which every provider does worst.

On the remaining objectives nearly everyone passes. All eighteen pass on operations
and on the environment, and all but one pass on technology and on security.
