\chapter{Provider Evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

The four studies in \cref{ch:methodology} rank the providers differently. STACKIT
scores highest on sovereignty, Deutsche Telekom's platform offers the most
services, OVHcloud has the fastest machines among the European providers, and
STACKIT again has the largest financial commitment behind it. This chapter
discusses the results of each study in turn, explains why the providers rank as
they do, and then combines the four studies to identify the strongest candidates
for an enterprise such as Alps Alpine.

% ============================================================
\section{Study 1 --- Sovereignty Scoring}
\label{sec:study1}

\subsection{What the questions reveal}
\label{subsec:study1-results}

Only five of the eighteen providers clear every minimum the framework sets:
STACKIT, Cyso Cloud, Cleura, Elastx and plusserver (\cref{tab:sov-seals}). The
rest mostly fall in the same two columns of that table, the legal objective and
the supply chain, and the reason only becomes clear one level down, in the
answers to the individual questions.

Those answers divide the market cleanly in two. Wherever a question concerns how
a provider runs its own business, nearly every provider answers well. Almost all
of them use open interfaces, let customers leave without being locked in, staff
their operations from within Europe, report security breaches under European
rules, and invest and employ in Europe. On everything a provider can decide for
itself, the European market is already in good shape.

The trouble starts lower down, among the machines, where the questions turn from
how a service is run to what it is built from. There the positive answers thin
out quickly and on some questions disappear altogether. No provider could show
that it is free of reliance on vendors outside Europe, and none that its supply
chain can be audited from end to end.

No provider runs on firmware of European origin, the low-level code built into
every server to control its hardware, and none is free of high-performance chips
made outside the Union. All eighteen answered no to both. Because every provider
gives the same answer, the cause is not any single provider but the European
industry that supplies them. The European Union states the same in its Chips Act,
which names a strong dependence on countries outside the Union for both the
design and the manufacture of chips \cite{EU_ChipsAct_2023}. The results of this
study are therefore consistent with the Union's own assessment.

OVHcloud, the only provider to answer yes on hardware, shows the extent of this
dependence. It builds its own servers at a factory in Croix in northern France, which no
other provider in the set does, yet the motherboards, processors, memory and
drives that go into them are bought from other suppliers
\cite{NextInk_OVHCroix_2023}. Building servers in Europe is a real achievement,
and the framework credits it, but the parts inside still come from elsewhere, and
OVHcloud answers no on firmware like everyone else.

This dependence also explains why no provider reaches above the middle of the
scale on the supply chain, or the top level on technology. As long as Europe
produces neither its own chips nor its own firmware, no provider can reach these
levels, and the strongest providers are already at the highest level currently
possible.
Artificial intelligence sits in the same place. No provider could show that its
\acs{AI} models are built, trained and governed entirely under European control.

Between what every provider does and what none can do lies a narrower band, the
questions only a handful answered yes although the answer lies in each provider's
own hands. UpCloud alone keeps all its critical suppliers and subcontractors under
European law. Four providers can keep running securely if an authority outside the
Union cuts off a supplier's support, four can prove that a customer's deleted data
is gone for good, and two run energy-efficient data centres against published
targets. None of this depends on chips or firmware. It depends on how a provider
chooses its partners, prepares for being cut off and records what it does, and for
most providers it is the only ground left within their reach.

That ground would be enough. Every provider that fails could clear all the
minimums by improving answers within its own control, and none is held back by
the chips and the firmware. For some the distance is short. Hetzner fails on a
single objective, the protection of data, and one better answer would carry it
through. noris network fails only on the supply chain, where buying part of its
servers from a trusted source would suffice. Scaleway is two steps away, one on
the legal side and one on the supply chain. Had these three closed those few gaps,
eight providers would pass instead of five.

For the large providers the distance is far greater, and almost all of it lies in
the law. OVHcloud and T-Cloud Public would need nearly every legal answer to turn
to yes, and the circumstance behind that is a business reaching well beyond
Europe. OVHcloud runs subsidiaries in the United States and Canada, and its
American arm states plainly that a request under the \acs{CLOUD Act} could include
data stored outside the United States \cite{OVHUS_CloudAct}. That exposure has
already been tested. A Canadian court has ordered OVHcloud to hand the Canadian
police customer data held on servers in France, Britain and Australia, over the
company's objection that French law forbids it, and OVHcloud has appealed
\cite{Heise_OVHCanada_2025}. IONOS has an American subsidiary in Philadelphia
\cite{UnitedInternet_AR_2025}, and Deutsche Telekom now earns about two thirds of
its revenue in the United States \cite{DT_AR_2025}. Hetzner's one weak objective
carries a trace of the same pattern, since its cloud offers locations in the
United States and Singapore on the same platform as its European ones
\cite{Hetzner_Locations}, and data stays in Europe only if the customer chooses
so.

This connects the sovereignty results with the financial study. Expanding abroad
is one way a provider grows its revenue, and \cref{fig:europe-share} shows that
OVHcloud and Deutsche Telekom have done so. Each expansion outside Europe,
however, makes the provider subject to another country's laws, which lowers its
result on the legal objective. For a European provider, international growth and
sovereignty therefore work against each other.

\subsection{The Huawei Problem and Deutsche Telekom's Response}
\label{subsec:huawei}

Deutsche Telekom appears twice in the results, and its two entries show the lower
layers at work more clearly than any other case. Open Telekom Cloud went live in
2016 on hardware and software from Huawei \cite{OTC_GoesLive_2016}, under an
agreement of 2015 in which Huawei supplies both \cite{LightReading_DT_2025}. Its
owner is German and its data centres stand in Germany, yet its equipment comes
from a company in a country whose laws, as \cref{subsec:other-jurisdictions}
described, can require organisations to cooperate with state intelligence work.
It is the only provider in the set with nothing at all on the supply chain, and it
finishes last overall.

Renamed T-Cloud Public, the same platform climbs twenty points to the middle of
\cref{fig:sov-ranking}. Deutsche Telekom presents it as \enquote{the next
evolution of the Open Telekom Cloud} and assures existing customers that
\enquote{nothing changes regarding the services they are used to}
\cite{TCloudPublic_2026}. \Cref{tab:otc-tcloud} shows where the platform moved up.

The biggest step, on strategy, has little to do with the platform. It rests on
Deutsche Telekom as a company, on who controls it, where its money comes from and
its part in European sovereignty initiatives, and the carbon reporting behind the
environmental step is published for the group as a whole. All of it was just as
true when the cloud still carried its old name.

The operational and legal steps rest on firmer ground, the way the platform is
run. Only T-Systems employees manage it. Huawei supplies the cloud operating
system but has no access to the hardware or software while it runs, its
engineers help only by video conference when called on, and T-Systems reviews
every fix before it is installed \cite{TCloudPublic_SecureCloud}. The step on the
supply chain comes from the hardware, now bought from Western manufacturers as
well as from Huawei, with processors and graphics cards from Intel and Nvidia
\cite{TCloudPublic_SecureCloud,LightReading_DT_2025}. For its newer Industrial
\acs{AI} Cloud in Munich, Deutsche Telekom went further and left Huawei out
altogether \cite{LightReading_DT_2026}.

The dates tell a different story from the rename. T-Systems described this way of
running the platform, and its Western hardware, on a page dated July 2020, four
years after launch and long before the new name \cite{TCloudPublic_SecureCloud}.
Most of what lifted the score was therefore already in place, and already
published, while the platform was still Open Telekom Cloud. One answer even moved
backwards: customer oversight of security monitoring was rated yes under the old
name and only partly under the new. The twenty points record above all how much
more clearly Deutsche Telekom now describes its platform. The Huawei operating
system at its core is still in use.

For providers that run software from outside Europe, Deutsche Telekom's path
still holds a lesson. The way a platform is operated can make up for part of
what it is built from. Keeping the foreign supplier away from the running
system, staffing operations with European employees and checking every update
lifted its legal and operational standing without replacing a single component,
and buying hardware from more than one source moved its supply chain off the
floor. Saying all of this openly mattered as well, because the framework can only
credit what it can see. The same fact should make buyers careful: a score can
rise while the platform stays the same, and comparing the two Deutsche Telekom
entries comes down to comparing two descriptions of one system. Providers that
already pass, such as STACKIT, Cyso Cloud and Cleura, gain little from this path,
because what separates them from the top of the scale is the firmware and the
chips that no European provider can yet replace.

\subsection{Interpreting the sovereignty scores}
\label{subsec:study1-interpretation}

A buyer who simply picks the highest score in \cref{fig:sov-ranking} can pick
wrongly. noris network scores above plusserver, yet noris network fails a
minimum supply chain sovereignty objective (SOV-5) and plusserver passes all objectives. For a regulated workload the pass decides, and the
score only orders the providers that have already passed.

The providers that pass are, apart from STACKIT, smaller and mostly independent
companies, while the large European names, OVHcloud, IONOS and Deutsche Telekom,
all fall short somewhere. A European address and a large business do not by
themselves make a provider sovereign. And because the scores measure only what a
provider shows from outside, a low score can reflect a provider that explains
itself poorly as much as one that protects data poorly, as
\cref{sec:design-limitations} noted and the Deutsche Telekom case has shown. The
scores make a sound shortlist, and the questions they leave open are the ones a
buyer still has to put to each provider on it.

\section{Study 2 --- Capability Gap Analysis}
\label{sec:study2}

\subsection{How far the catalogue reaches}
\label{subsec:study2-results}

No European provider can yet take over everything Alps Alpine runs on \acs{AWS}
(\cref{fig:readiness-ranking}). Deutsche Telekom's platform comes closest,
covering a little under three quarters of the catalogue. OVHcloud covers just
over half, and STACKIT and Scaleway well under half. After those four the bars
drop sharply, and the remaining twelve providers cover around a quarter each.

The gaps are far from even (\cref{tab:readiness-matrix}). Plain computing power
is where the European providers come closest to \acs{AWS}, with storage and
networking at about half. Artificial intelligence and web and app hosting are
almost empty columns, and six services, the managed language-model service among
them, are offered by no provider at all. The leading providers in
\cref{fig:readiness-radar} trace the same shape, bulging towards \acs{AWS} on
computing and networking and collapsing inwards on artificial intelligence,
messaging and the developer tools.

\subsection{What lies behind the gap}
\label{subsec:study2-interpretation}

The shape of the gap reflects the kind of companies these are. European providers
sell the basic building blocks well, the machines, disks and networks. What they
mostly lack are the finished services built on top, the ones that spare a
customer the work of building and running things itself, and
\cref{fig:readiness-verdicts} shows how many of those are missing even at the
leading providers. These finished services are where a customer's own effort is
invested, so they are also where moving becomes hard. A plain server has an
equivalent everywhere. A system built around a managed \acs{AWS} service with no
European counterpart has to be rebuilt in that part before it can move at all.

Much of this gap could close quickly, because much of it has already been closed
somewhere in Europe. If each provider added only the services that some other
European provider already offers in full, every one of the sixteen would arrive at
roughly three quarters of the catalogue, almost exactly where Deutsche Telekom's
platform stands today. The smaller providers would gain the most, above all in the
Internet of Things, messaging and the developer tools, where one or two European
providers have already shown it can be done. The European market as a whole
already covers about three quarters of what Alps Alpine uses. What no single
provider offers, the providers between them largely do.

The last quarter is another matter. Twenty-four of the sixty services are offered
in full by no European provider, and six of them not even in part, among them the
managed key-value and time-series databases, the high-performance relational
database, workflow orchestration and the managed language-model service. Closing
that quarter means building what Europe does not yet have, not copying what a
neighbour already sells. Most of it is software, which a provider can build given
money and time, and \cref{sec:study3} turns to that money. Artificial intelligence
is harder. Its services depend on specialised high-performance processors, and
dependence on such processors from outside the Union is one of the two questions
that every provider failed in Study~1. The weakest area of the catalogue and the
hardest limit on sovereignty sit in the same layer of the stack. For Alps Alpine,
whose search for alternatives began with public \acs{AI} services, this matters
more than any other result of the study: the work it most wants to move is the
work for which the European offer is thinnest.

\section{Study 3 --- Financial Viability}
\label{sec:study3}

\subsection{The money and the progress}
\label{subsec:study3-results}

More money has not bought more capability (\cref{fig:capital-gap}). T-Cloud
Public reaches the highest capability on the smallest commitment of the five,
while STACKIT, with by far the largest, sits in the middle. The reason is that
most of the money has not yet become anything a customer can use. Three quarters
of the European commitments sit inside deadlines that have not yet arrived, with
STACKIT's new campus at L\"ubbenau due only in phases from 2027
(\cref{sec:study3-investment}), and some of what was counted bought something
other than cloud services, such as the security software company that the Schwarz
Group acquired. IONOS shows the opposite case, a large commitment behind a
business that mostly sells hosting and domains, so its capability stays low
however much is spent. Capability follows what the money has already built and
what the provider chooses to build with it, not the size of the cheque. The money also comes
from two quite different places. The bars for OVHcloud and IONOS in
\cref{fig:capital-structure} are made of loans and of shares sold to investors,
while those for STACKIT, Scaleway and T-Cloud Public are filled almost entirely by
their owners.

The pace of progress varies just as widely (\cref{fig:parity-projection}).
STACKIT, the youngest provider, has the steepest line of all and meets the
\acs{AWS} line around 2031, a year after T-Cloud Public. OVHcloud and Scaleway
arrive only around 2040, and IONOS not within any period a contract would cover.

\subsection{The Revenue Chasm and Capital Efficiency}
\label{subsec:revenue-chasm}

The catch-up years in \cref{fig:parity-projection} treat \acs{AWS} as standing
still at the catalogue it offered in 2026, and \cref{fig:global-revenue-common}
shows that it does not. Over ten years Amazon's line climbs steeply away from
every other owner, and the dashed line for its cloud business alone rises until,
by 2025, it nearly matches the whole of Deutsche Telekom. The owners of Scaleway,
IONOS and OVHcloud remain at the bottom of the chart throughout. Over the decade
the gap has grown, not narrowed.

A company that earns so much more each year can also spend far more each year on
new services, so the catalogue the European providers are chasing keeps moving
away from them. Matching \acs{AWS} service for service is beyond the reach of any
of them. Covering the particular services one customer needs is not, because that
list is far shorter, and the two fastest providers, STACKIT and T-Cloud Public,
could plausibly cover it within the life of a contract signed today.

Where the money comes from matters for sovereignty as much as how much there is.
A provider funded by investors may one day belong to whoever buys its shares,
while a provider funded by its owner lives on that owner's continued interest.
STACKIT is the clearest case of the second kind: almost all of its committed money
rests on announcements by one privately held company that publishes no figures
for its cloud business \cite{Schwarz_Figures_FY2025}. Deutsche Telekom shows
another side of the same question. Its line in \cref{fig:europe-share} falls
steadily, and the group now earns about two thirds of its revenue in the United
States \cite{DT_AR_2025}, even though its cloud platform is European.

\subsection{What this means for a long contract}
\label{subsec:study3-interpretation}

All five providers are either sizeable companies in their own right or part of
much larger groups, and none looks likely to disappear within a five-year
contract. They differ instead in how much a customer can see and in whose
decision their future rests on. OVHcloud is the most open of the five. Its cloud
business is the whole company, its accounts are public, and its risk, which lies
with its lenders and shareholders, is in plain view. STACKIT is the least open but
has the deepest backing, and its risk lies in a single owner's continued
commitment. T-Cloud Public and Scaleway sit between the two. OVHcloud and STACKIT
are therefore both sound choices for a long commitment, for opposite reasons, and
choosing between them means choosing which kind of risk a buyer would rather keep
an eye on.

\section{Study 4 --- Technical Benchmarking}
\label{sec:study4}

\subsection{Speed on the same machine}
\label{subsec:study4-results}

On machines of the same size, the European providers keep pace with \acs{AWS}
(\cref{fig:bench-composite}). The six bars sit close together, and OVHcloud's is
almost as long as that of \acs{AWS}. The heatmap in \cref{tab:bench-heatmap}
shows how the order arises. OVHcloud holds the best result in most columns, and
four European providers beat \acs{AWS} when all processor cores work together.
\acs{AWS} owes its first place to a single column, the network, where its cell is
the only deep green and every European provider falls into the warmer colours.
Without that column OVHcloud would come first.

\subsection{Price and Performance}
\label{subsec:price-performance}

Once price is counted, the order almost turns upside down (\cref{fig:bench-value}).
IONOS, the cheapest, gives the most performance for the money, more than twice
what \acs{AWS} gives, with OVHcloud next. T-Cloud Public, the most expensive
machine in the test, gives the least. OVHcloud matches or beats \acs{AWS} on most
tests for a little over half its hourly price.

OVHcloud's long bar in the disk test (\cref{fig:bench-postmark}) has a particular
cause. Its disk sits inside the same server, while every other provider,
\acs{AWS} included, keeps the disk on a separate storage system reached over the
network (\cref{sec:bench-disk}). The local disk is faster, but the network disk is
easier to copy and protect. The two are different products, and the faster one is
not simply the better.

\subsection{What lies behind the measurements}
\label{subsec:study4-interpretation}

The gaps of the earlier studies vanish here because a single machine cannot show
them. Europe's cloud providers can build a fast server and sell it cheaply. What
they cannot yet offer is the range of services around it and the scale behind it,
which is what Studies~2 and~3 measured.

The network is the one exception (\cref{fig:bench-iperf}). \acs{AWS} transfers
data between machines considerably faster than every European provider, and many
times faster than Scaleway. A single application on one machine would hardly notice, but a
system spread across many machines that constantly exchange data would, and
systems of that kind are common in large enterprises. These results describe one
size of machine measured over a short period (\cref{sec:bench-findings}). European
performance is competitive today; whether it stays so, and whether every machine a
customer rents performs alike, the measurements cannot say.

\section{The Sovereignty--Readiness Trade-off}
\label{sec:tradeoff}

Set side by side, the sovereignty ranking in \cref{fig:sov-ranking} and the
capability ranking in \cref{fig:readiness-ranking} run in almost opposite
directions. Of the four providers with the broadest catalogues, only STACKIT
passes the sovereignty minimums, and the other four that pass sit among the
providers offering the fewest services. Deutsche Telekom's platform offers the
most and, under its old name, scored the least on sovereignty. noris network sits
near the top of one chart and near the bottom of the other.

The two ends of the pattern explain it. The platform with the broadest catalogue
was built on Huawei's hardware and software
\cite{OTC_GoesLive_2016,LightReading_DT_2025}, and the providers that pass every
minimum are, STACKIT apart, smaller companies offering far less. Beneath both lies
the same limit, the servers, chips and firmware at the bottom of the stack that no
European provider makes for itself (\cref{sec:full-stack}). It is the layer that
leaves artificial intelligence almost empty in Study~2, and the one that keeps
every provider below the top of the sovereignty scale, so the trade-off and the
capability gap have the same cause.

STACKIT shows that the trade-off is real but not fixed. It leads on sovereignty,
comes third on services and has added services faster than any other provider in
the set, so a provider can grow without giving up control. It has not yet shown
that a sovereign provider can reach the breadth an enterprise like Alps Alpine
needs, because it still covers less than half of it. The realistic choice for an
enterprise is therefore not one provider over another but a balance struck for
each kind of work: sensitive data where sovereignty is strongest, accepting a
shorter list of services there, and less sensitive work where the services are
broadest.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{subsec:sensitivity}

The top of the order shifts with what a buyer values most, as the right-hand
columns of \cref{tab:combined-ranking} show. A buyer who puts services first
would choose T-Cloud Public, with OVHcloud rising to second; a buyer who puts
sovereignty first would choose STACKIT; and when performance matters most the
first three are almost tied. The bottom of the order never moves. Scaleway stays
fourth and IONOS fifth whatever the buyer values. Judged by its old name rather
than its new one, Deutsche Telekom's platform falls from first to third, a
reminder of how much its position rests on the way it is described.

The sovereignty minimums were set for this study rather than by the framework
(\cref{tab:sov-objectives}), so each was raised and lowered in turn. A slightly
looser bar on data or on the supply chain would let in Hetzner or noris network,
the two providers already closest to passing. A stricter bar on strategy,
technology or security would remove some of the five that pass, and a stricter bar
on data or the supply chain would remove every one of them, because of the limit
described in \cref{subsec:study1-results}. A looser legal bar on its
own lets in nobody, because every provider that fails on law also fails somewhere
else. Through all of these changes STACKIT is never excluded unless every provider
is.

Taken together, the four studies lead to a consistent result. STACKIT and T-Cloud Public
are the two strongest European candidates, with OVHcloud close behind, and which
of the first two leads depends on what a buyer values most. Only STACKIT meets the
sovereignty requirements, under every weighting and every reasonable reading of
the framework. In every study, the main limitation is the same: Europe does not
yet produce its own chips and firmware, which limits both how sovereign any
provider can become and how many services it can offer.
