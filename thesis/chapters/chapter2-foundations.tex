\chapter{Foundations}
\label{ch:foundations}

When an enterprise asks a cloud provider where its data will be kept, the answer
usually comes as a place: a data centre in Frankfurt, in Paris, in Amsterdam. It
is tempting to stop there and conclude that data kept in Europe is protected by
European law. This chapter explains why that conclusion does not hold. It begins
with what sovereignty actually means, follows the question through the laws of
Europe, the United States and China, and then down through the layers of
technology on which every cloud service is built. Each step adds a reason why the
location of a data centre is only the beginning of the answer, and together they
explain how the providers are assessed in the chapters that follow.

\section{Data Sovereignty: Definition and Dimensions}
\label{sec:sovereignty-definition}

The term \emph{data sovereignty} is used in more than one sense. Cloud providers
mostly use it to mean the country in which data is stored. European policy uses it
more broadly. It covers the confidentiality of European data, but also the
strength of European providers and the wish to depend less on foreign ones
\cite{Blancato_CloudSovereigntyNexus_2024}. The two meanings can lead to opposite
conclusions about the same provider, so the term needs to be pinned down before it
is used to judge anyone.

\subsection{Four Distinct Properties}
\label{subsec:four-properties}

What people call sovereignty is really four different properties, and a provider
can have one without the others. \Cref{tab:sovereignty-dimensions} sets them out,
each with the question it answers and what decides it.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Four properties commonly grouped under the term
           \enquote{sovereignty}, the question each answers, and what actually
           determines it.}
  \label{tab:sovereignty-dimensions}
  \small
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Property} & \textbf{Question it answers} & \textbf{Determined by} \\
    \midrule
    Data residency
      & In which territory is the data stored and processed?
      & Facility location \\[0.4em]
    Data sovereignty
      & Which jurisdiction may lawfully compel its disclosure?
      & Corporate incorporation \\[0.4em]
    Operational sovereignty
      & Who is technically able to access or administer the system?
      & Staffing and key control \\[0.4em]
    Technological sovereignty
      & Can the stack be inspected, modified and operated independently?
      & Supply chain and licensing \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The easiest of the four to show is \textbf{data residency}, which is simply a
matter of geography. A provider meets it by keeping and processing data within a
given territory, and can prove it with the address of its data centre. This is the
property most providers advertise.

\textbf{Data sovereignty} is about something else: which country's authorities can
lawfully order a provider to hand the data over. That depends not on where the
servers stand but on where the operating company and its parent are registered.

Even a provider registered in Europe may not keep data out of reach if the wrong
people can get to it. \textbf{Operational sovereignty} asks who can actually
access and run the system. If a provider's support engineers can log in from
abroad, or if the provider rather than the customer holds the keys that unlock the
data, the data is not beyond outside reach, however well the first two properties
are met.

Beneath all three lies \textbf{technological sovereignty}, which asks whether the
hardware, firmware and software can be inspected, changed and run without
depending on a single foreign supplier. \Cref{sec:full-stack} returns to this
property, because it turns out to be the hardest for European providers to meet.

\subsection{Why Location Alone Does Not Guarantee Sovereignty}
\label{subsec:location-jurisdiction}

The belief that data kept in a country is safe from foreign authorities breaks
down when the provider's home country claims power over its subsidiaries abroad.
A data centre in Frankfurt run by the European subsidiary of an American company
keeps the data in Germany, yet the American parent remains subject to American
orders to disclose data, including data held abroad.

This is not a theoretical worry, as a case involving Microsoft shows
(\cref{fig:microsoft-ireland}). In December 2013 American federal agents obtained
a warrant under the Stored Communications Act requiring Microsoft to hand over the
contents of a customer's email account, as part of a drug-trafficking
investigation \cite{MicrosoftIreland_SCOTUS_2018}. The emails were stored in one
place only, Microsoft's data centre in Dublin, and Microsoft asked the court to cancel
the warrant for that data. The first judge refused, a higher judge of the same court
agreed and punished Microsoft for not handing the emails over, and the Court of Appeals then
reversed both, ruling that forcing disclosure of data stored in Ireland would
stretch the law beyond the borders of the United States
\cite{MicrosoftIreland_2dCir_2016}.

For a moment, then, the location of the data seemed to matter.
But on 23~March 2018, while the case was waiting before the Supreme Court, Congress
passed the \ac{CLOUD Act} \cite{CLOUDAct_2018}. It changed the Stored
Communications Act so that a provider must preserve, back up or disclose data in
its possession, custody or control \enquote{regardless of whether such
communication, record, or other information is located within or outside of the
United States}. The government obtained a new warrant under the amended law, and
the Supreme Court sent the case back to be dismissed, since there was nothing left
to decide \cite{MicrosoftIreland_SCOTUS_2018}.

Keeping data in Europe is therefore necessary, but it is not enough. Any assessment
that treats the two as the same will rate providers with a parent outside the
\ac{EU} as more sovereign than they are. \Cref{sec:legal-landscape} looks at the
laws behind this, and \cref{sec:study1} shows what it means for the providers in
this thesis.

\subsection{The Definition Adopted in This Work}
\label{subsec:definition-adopted}

In this thesis sovereignty is treated as all four properties of
\cref{tab:sovereignty-dimensions} together. To turn that into something that can be
measured, the thesis adopts the European Commission's Cloud Sovereignty Framework
\cite{EC_DIGIT_CloudSovereignty_2025}, which breaks sovereignty into eight weighted
objectives: strategic, legal, data, operational, supply-chain, technological,
security and environmental.

This framework was chosen instead of developing a new one, for two reasons. The
framework comes from the institution that sets European public-sector procurement
policy, so the scores it produces mean something outside this study. And its
objectives already keep the four properties apart, so a provider cannot make up
for a weak legal position with a strong showing on data location. How the
framework is structured and scored is described in \cref{sec:method-study1}.

\section{Legal and Regulatory Landscape}
\label{sec:legal-landscape}

The Microsoft case shows that the question of who can reach data is decided by
law, and not by one law but by several. The United States, the European Union and
China have each written rules about data, for different purposes, and where those
rules meet they often pull against each other: what one country orders a provider
to do, another may forbid. A provider working across them can be caught between
duties it cannot meet at the same time, and an enterprise that chooses that
provider takes on the same conflict.


\subsection{Regulations versus Directives}
\label{subsec:regulations-directives}

The European Union makes law in two main forms, and the difference decides whether
a rule is the same everywhere in Europe or different in each country.

A \textbf{regulation} becomes law in every member state on the day it takes
effect. No national parliament has to act and no national law has to be written.
The rule that applies in Germany is the same rule, in the same words, that applies
in France and in Ireland.

A \textbf{directive} works differently. It sets a goal that every member state must
reach and a deadline by which each must pass its own law to reach it. Twenty-seven
countries therefore produce twenty-seven laws. They share a purpose, but they can
differ in scope, in detail and in their penalties, and each takes effect only when
its country has passed it. The Union's founding treaty draws exactly this line,
describing a regulation as \enquote{binding in its entirety and directly
applicable} in all member states, and a directive as \enquote{binding, as to the
result to be achieved} while leaving \enquote{to the national authorities the
choice of form and methods} \cite{TFEU_Art288}. \Cref{fig:eu-instruments} shows the
two paths side by side.

Most of these laws are regulations, and that is a deliberate choice: legislating by
regulation is how the Union avoids the patchwork that directives create. Two of
them touch the questions of this thesis directly. \Ac{DORA} \cite{DORA_2022}
places critical cloud providers under direct supervision, and the Data Act
\cite{DataAct_2023} gives customers the right to switch from one provider to
another, which matters for the question of lock-in discussed in
\cref{ch:conclusion}. The \ac{CRA} \cite{CRA_2024} reaches further down, setting
rules for the products and components that cloud infrastructure is built from
(\cref{sec:full-stack}).

For this research it follows that \enquote{\ac{EU} compliance} is not one thing a
provider either has or lacks. Where a duty comes from a regulation, the answer is
the same everywhere in Europe. Where it comes from a directive, it depends on the
countries in which the provider and its customer operate, and in some of them the
duty may not yet be in force at all. This is one reason the providers in this
thesis are measured against the Commission's own framework
(\cref{subsec:definition-adopted}) and not against national laws that differ from
country to country.

\subsection{GDPR and the Schrems II Ruling}
\label{subsec:gdpr-schrems}

Of all these laws, the \ac{GDPR} \cite{GDPR2016} has done most to shape the
question of where data may go. As a regulation it applies in the same form in
every member state. It governs how personal data may be collected, stored and
used, and it binds every organisation that handles the personal data of people in
the Union, wherever that organisation is based.

One part of it matters especially for cloud services: the rules on sending
personal data outside the Union. The reason for them is simple. The rights that
protect data inside Europe would mean little if the data could be moved to a
country where those rights do not apply, so a transfer abroad is lawful only if
the protection travels with the data. The Regulation offers three ways to show that
it does \cite{GDPR2016}, and \cref{fig:gdpr-transfer} shows how a company works
through them.

The simplest route is an \emph{adequacy decision}. The Commission examines a
country's laws, finds that they protect personal data about as well as the
Union's, and data may then flow there as freely as to another member state. Where
there is no such decision, the recipient can sign a contract built on standard
clauses published by the Commission, committing itself to European standards. A
third route, a narrow exception such as the explicit consent of the person
concerned, exists for particular cases and is not meant for everyday transfers.

The first two routes were both tested against the law of the United States, in a
dispute brought by one man that lasted most of a decade. Maximillian Schrems, an
Austrian Facebook user, complained to the Irish data protection authority that his
data, sent by Facebook Ireland to servers in the United States, was not protected
from American authorities. His complaint was rejected, because the Commission had
already declared the United States adequate under an arrangement called Safe
Harbour. In 2015 the Court of Justice declared that decision invalid
\cite{SchremsI_2015}. Schrems complained again, this time about transfers made
under the standard contract clauses, and by the time his case returned to the Court
the Commission had issued a new adequacy decision, the \ac{EU}--US Privacy Shield.

In 2020 the Court struck that down as well \cite{SchremsII_2020}. American
surveillance law, it found, let government agencies reach the data far more freely
than European law allows, and a European whose data ended up there had no
practical way to challenge it, because American courts were not open to them. The
standard contract clauses survived, so a company could still send personal data to
the United States under a contract. What it could no longer do was rely on the
Commission's word that the country was safe.

The deeper lesson of the judgment lies in what the \ac{GDPR} can and cannot do. It
binds those who handle personal data and the contracts between them, but it does
not bind foreign governments. When a country's law lets its authorities reach data
in ways Europe considers excessive, no contract can change that, because a contract
binds only the parties who sign it. The Court therefore placed the burden on the
company sending the data, which must now examine the law of the destination country
before transferring, and stop if the protection cannot be guaranteed.

For an enterprise choosing a cloud provider, this means that reading the contract
is not enough. However carefully it is written, a contract cannot stop a government
from ordering the provider to hand data over. What decides the outcome is which
country's law the provider answers to (\cref{subsec:location-jurisdiction}), and
what that law allows its government to demand.

\subsection{Extraterritorial Reach: CLOUD Act and FISA 702}
\label{subsec:cloud-act}

The law of the United States gives its government two separate ways to obtain data
held by an American company. They work very differently, and only one of them
matters greatly to a European enterprise. \Cref{tab:us-powers} sets them side by
side.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Two routes by which United States authorities can reach data held by
           an American provider.}
  \label{tab:us-powers}
  \small
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l X X@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{\acs{CLOUD Act}} & \textbf{FISA section 702} \\
    \midrule
    Purpose          & Criminal investigation & Foreign intelligence \\[0.35em]
    Who is targeted  & A named account in a specific case
                     & People who are not United States citizens and are
                       outside the United States \\[0.35em]
    Customer informed & Sometimes & No \\[0.35em]
    Provider can contest & Yes, in open court & Limited, and in secret \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The first is the \ac{CLOUD Act}, the law that ended the Microsoft case. It serves
criminal investigations. A prosecutor obtains a warrant for a named account, and
the provider must produce what it holds. The provider knows about the order and
can challenge it in court, as Microsoft did.

The second is section~702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act
\cite{FISA702}, which serves intelligence gathering rather than prosecution. It
allows the surveillance of people who are not American citizens and are believed
to be outside the United States, it requires providers to help, and it forbids
them to tell the customer concerned.

It is this second route that matters more to a European enterprise, because its
company, its employees and its customers are exactly the people section~702 is
aimed at: they are not American citizens and they are not in the United States. A
criminal warrant is a rare event that may never come, but falling within the
target group of a foreign intelligence programme is a permanent condition.

Neither power depends on where the servers stand. The \ac{CLOUD Act} reaches data
within a provider's possession, custody or control, which is a question of what the
company is able to obtain, not of where the data is kept. If an American parent can
instruct its European subsidiary to retrieve data, or holds passwords that reach
into the subsidiary's systems, the data is within its control. Setting up a
European subsidiary therefore does not solve the problem on its own. What decides
it is the chain of ownership and the lines of access: who can give orders to whom,
and which staff and systems can reach the data. A subsidiary that is wholly owned
by an American parent, run by staff who report to it, or dependent on its systems
for support remains within reach.

European law saw this situation coming. Under the \ac{GDPR}, an order from a
foreign court or authority demanding personal data is not by itself a lawful
reason for a European company to hand it over \cite{GDPR2016}. Such an order counts
only when the two countries have a treaty covering requests of that kind. These
treaties exist, and they send the request from one government to the other, which
then obtains the data through its own courts. A warrant served directly on the
provider skips that route entirely, as \cref{fig:law-collision} shows, and under
European law it is not a valid reason to disclose anything.

A provider caught by both systems is then stuck. American law tells it to hand the
data over, and European law tells it that the order is no reason to do so. Whichever
it obeys, it breaks the other, and neither the provider nor its customer can
resolve the conflict. This is why a provider cannot be judged on where its data
centres are, since neither power turns on that. It has to be judged on where it is
registered, who owns it and who can reach its systems, which is what the assessment
in \cref{sec:method-study1} sets out to measure.

\subsection{Government Pressure on AI Providers}
\label{subsec:ai-providers}

The Microsoft case and the Facebook case of Maximillian Schrems were both about the
same thing: who is allowed to look at someone's data. In each, a government wanted
access, a company or a citizen pushed back, and judges had the final word. In 2026
a new kind of conflict appeared. This time a government and a technology company
fought not over data, but over what an artificial intelligence system should be
allowed to do.

The company was Anthropic, the American firm that makes the Claude \acs{AI}
models, and the government, through its Department of
Defense, which the administration now also calls the Department of War. In
February 2026 the two were negotiating how the military could use Claude. Anthropic
was willing to allow almost every use, but it drew two lines. It would not let its
models be used to watch the population of the United States on a mass scale, and it
would not let them power weapons that choose and attack targets on their own,
without a person making the decision. According to Anthropic, the Department
answered with threats: to throw the company out of its systems, to brand it a
\enquote{supply chain risk}, a label until then kept for suppliers from hostile
countries, and to use an emergency law, the Defense Production Act, to force the
limits to be removed \cite{Anthropic_DoW_Statement_2026}.

Anthropic did not give way, and the talks collapsed. The President told every
federal agency to stop using Anthropic's technology, allowing the military six
months to remove it from the systems where it was already in use
\cite{AlJazeera_OpenAI_Pentagon_2026}. The Department then carried out its threat
and labelled Anthropic a supply chain risk, which shut Claude out of defence work,
including work done by the Department's many contractors
\cite{AP_Anthropic_DCCircuit_2026}. Within hours of the talks breaking down, its
competitor OpenAI announced that it had reached its own agreement with the
Department. OpenAI said it had kept the same two limits, while its chief executive
admitted that the deal had been \enquote{definitely rushed}
\cite{AlJazeera_OpenAI_Pentagon_2026,TechCrunch_OpenAI_Pentagon_2026}.

Anthropic took the government to court, and the judges have not agreed. A federal
judge in San Francisco found that the Pentagon had acted illegally in punishing the
company, but in September 2026 an appeals court in Washington decided, by two
judges to one, that the Pentagon was entitled to treat Anthropic as a supply chain
risk under a different law \cite{AP_Anthropic_DCCircuit_2026}. As this thesis is
written, the conflict is still open.

All of this happened between an American government and American companies, yet it
matters to anyone in Europe who relies on American \acs{AI}. It shows that the
terms on which such a service is offered are not decided by the provider and its
customers alone. A provider can be pushed to drop its own limits, can lose a large
part of its market overnight, and can find a competitor in its place within hours,
all because of a dispute that its other customers had no say in. In the earlier
cases the question was who could reach the data. Here the question was who decides
what the technology may do, and the answer, again, depended on the provider's home
country. For Alps Alpine the lesson comes close to home, because its search for
alternatives began with \acs{AI} services, the very area in which, as
\cref{sec:study2} shows, European providers have the least to offer.

\subsection{China and Other Jurisdictions}
\label{subsec:other-jurisdictions}

It might seem that the simple answer is to avoid American providers. The picture is
not that simple, because other countries have their own rules about data crossing
their borders, and those rules do not all point the same way.

China restricts data leaving the country in much the same way Europe does. Under
its Data Security Law, data held in China may not be handed to a foreign law
enforcement authority without the approval of the Chinese government
\cite{ChinaDSL_2021}, and its Personal Information Protection Law says the same for
personal data \cite{ChinaPIPL_2021}. The reasoning is the one already met in
\cref{subsec:cloud-act}: a country that has gone to the trouble of protecting data
on its territory does not want a foreign government reaching past it. Europe and
China have therefore arrived at the same position, that a company may not hand data
to a foreign government outside an agreed channel. The United States stands apart,
with a law that reaches the other way, and a provider active in all three is caught
between rules built to resist one another.

China also has a law on cooperation with intelligence work. It requires
organisations to support, assist and cooperate in national intelligence work
\enquote{in accordance with the law}, and a later provision states that such work
must respect human rights and protect the lawful rights and interests of
organisations \cite{ChinaNIL_2017}. How far the obligation reaches in practice is
disputed, and the qualifying words are often left out when the provision is
quoted. This thesis takes no position on that dispute. What matters for an
assessment is that the obligation exists and that its limits are not settled.

None of the providers assessed in this thesis is Chinese-owned, so the question
does not arise through ownership. It arises through equipment. A European provider
that builds its infrastructure on hardware and firmware made elsewhere takes on
whatever questions come with that equipment, and it cannot answer them by pointing
to where it is registered.

By this point the reasons have accumulated. The Microsoft case showed that
location gives way to control, Schrems showed that contracts cannot bind foreign
governments, and the comparison of the three legal systems shows that a provider
cannot simply be sorted into European or not. An assessment has to ask where a
provider is registered, who owns it, whose equipment it runs and what each of those
countries can demand. That is why the framework adopted in
\cref{subsec:definition-adopted} scores law and supply chain as separate
objectives, and it is also where the next section begins: with the equipment
itself.

\section{Sovereignty as a Full-Stack Property}
\label{sec:full-stack}

A cloud service is not a single thing but a stack of layers, each resting on the
one below. At the bottom are the physical servers, with their processors, memory
and disks, and the firmware, the low-level software built into the hardware that
starts it and controls it. On top of the hardware runs an operating system, and on
top of that a hypervisor, the software that divides one physical server into many
virtual machines (\cref{sec:technical-foundations}). Around these sit the networks
that connect the machines, the storage systems that hold the data, and the
monitoring tools that record what happens on the platform. Only at the very top
is the customer's own application.

Each layer has power over the layers above it. The operating system decides what
the programs running on it may do, the hypervisor decides what each virtual
machine may see and use, and the firmware sits beneath all of them. The United
States standards body NIST describes platform firmware as critical to the
trustworthiness of a computing system and notes that much of it is highly
privileged, so that an attacker who changes it can hide malicious code that
survives beneath everything installed above it \cite{NIST_SP800193_2018}.

For a cloud customer this has a direct consequence. In the standard definition of
the most basic cloud service, renting virtual machines, the customer controls its
operating systems, storage and applications but \enquote{does not manage or
control the underlying cloud infrastructure} \cite{NIST_SP800145_2011}. Everything
below the customer's own machines belongs to the provider, and with it the
equipment, firmware and software the provider has chosen. An application can be
written, operated and stored entirely in Europe and still run on a hypervisor,
servers or firmware supplied from elsewhere. Its sovereignty can be no stronger
than that of the weakest layer beneath it.

This is why the framework adopted in this thesis does not stop at law and
ownership. It assesses the supply chain and the technology underneath a service
as objectives of their own (\cref{tab:sov-objectives}), asking where the
hardware is made, where the firmware comes from and whether the platform depends
on processors from outside the Union. \Cref{subsec:study1-results} shows that it
is at these lowest layers that every European provider in this study falls short.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Centralised.} In the centralised model, one company owns and runs the whole platform and
rents it to anyone who wants it. NIST calls this a public cloud, provisioned for
open use by the general public and existing on the premises of the cloud provider
\cite{NIST_SP800145_2011}. \acs{AWS}, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud follow this
model, and so do the European providers assessed in this thesis. The customer
gains a wide range of services and gives up control over everything beneath its
own machines, as \cref{sec:full-stack} described.

  \item \textbf{Decentralised.} The decentralised model removes the central operator altogether. Data and
work are spread across many independent computers that cooperate directly, with
no single owner. The file system IPFS is one example: a peer-to-peer system in
which, its designer writes, there is no single point of failure and nodes do not
need to trust each other \cite{Benet_IPFS_2014}. Bitcoin applied the same idea to
payments, so that they could be made without a trusted third party
\cite{Nakamoto_Bitcoin_2008}. No single company can then be ordered to hand data
over, but no single company is responsible for keeping the service running
either.

  \item \textbf{Federated.} The federated model sits between the two. Independent providers keep their
own platforms but agree on common rules, so that data and services can move
between them under shared conditions. Gaia-X, established in 2021 as a
European association, follows this approach. It is not a cloud provider itself and
offers no infrastructure or storage of its own; it describes itself as a trusted
federation of infrastructures and services \cite{GaiaX_FAQ}, and its architecture
defines the shared services and rules that such an ecosystem runs on
\cite{GAIAX_Architecture_2023}. Catena-X applies the same principles to one
industry. Founded in 2021, it is an open data ecosystem for the automotive
industry and complies with the Gaia-X rules \cite{CatenaX_Network_2026}.

  \item \textbf{Self-hosted.} In the self-hosted model, an organisation runs its own cloud for its own
use. NIST calls this a private cloud, provisioned for the exclusive use of a single
organisation, which may be located on or off the organisation's premises
\cite{NIST_SP800145_2011}. Control is as complete as it can be, because every layer
is chosen and operated by the organisation itself, but so is the work of building,
running and extending it.
\end{enumerate}

This thesis compares the centralised European providers with each other and with
\acs{AWS}, and includes the self-hosted option as a point of reference at the far
end of the range. Federated initiatives such as Gaia-X do not replace these
providers; they set common rules that the providers can choose to follow.

\section{Technical Foundations of Cloud Infrastructure}
\label{sec:technical-foundations}

Every computer separates the software that controls the machine from the
software that merely uses it. The processor offers different levels of privilege.
The operating system's core, the kernel, runs at the most privileged level, where
it may carry out every instruction, while ordinary applications run at a lower
level and are not allowed to carry out the instructions that control the hardware.
On the common x86 processors these levels are called rings, numbered from zero,
the most privileged, to three, and the kernel normally runs in ring zero because
no other ring can execute the privileged instructions \cite{Barham_Xen_2003}.
Popek and Goldberg described the same division as a supervisor mode, in which the
full set of instructions is available, and a user mode, in which it is not
\cite{Popek_Goldberg_1974}.

Virtualization builds on this division. A hypervisor, also called a virtual
machine monitor, places itself beneath the operating systems and gives each of them
what appears to be a machine of its own. Popek and Goldberg set out in 1974 what a
virtual machine must achieve to deserve the name. A program running in it must
behave as it would on the real machine, it must not be able to take control of
resources the hypervisor has not given it, and most of its instructions must run
directly on the hardware, so that it is not slowed down unnecessarily
\cite{Popek_Goldberg_1974}. The difficulty lies in the privileged instructions, which
the guest operating system expects to carry out itself but which only the
hypervisor may be allowed to execute.

Three techniques have been used to solve this. In \emph{full virtualization}, the
guest operating system runs unchanged, and the hypervisor rewrites its privileged
instructions as they are met, a technique known as binary translation. The VMware
products of the early 2000s worked this way \cite{Adams_Agesen_2006}. In
\emph{paravirtualization}, the guest operating system is modified so that it asks
the hypervisor directly instead of attempting privileged instructions. Xen took this
approach, accepting changes to the guest in return for an overhead its authors
measured at a few per cent at most \cite{Barham_Xen_2003}. In
\emph{hardware-assisted virtualization}, the processor itself supports the
hypervisor: Intel and AMD added extensions to their processors for this purpose,
so that unmodified guests can run without rewriting. The first generation of
these extensions was, surprisingly, rarely faster than the software techniques
it was meant to replace \cite{Adams_Agesen_2006}, but the hardware approach has
since become the foundation of modern hypervisors.

Hypervisors are also divided by where they run. A bare-metal, or native,
hypervisor runs directly on the hardware without a host operating system beneath
it, while a hosted hypervisor runs on top of an ordinary operating system, like
any other program \cite{NIST_SP800125_2011}. Cloud platforms use the bare-metal
kind, because it leaves less software between the virtual machines and the
hardware.

KVM, the Kernel-based Virtual Machine, combines the two ideas. Introduced in 2007,
it is a module of the Linux kernel that uses the processors' virtualization
extensions to turn Linux itself into a hypervisor, with each virtual machine
appearing to Linux as an ordinary process \cite{Kivity_KVM_2007}. Because it reuses
everything Linux already offers, from device drivers to memory management, KVM
spread widely. It is the default hypervisor of OpenStack \cite{OpenStack_Nova_KVM},
the open-source software on which several of the European providers in this thesis
build (\cref{sec:lockin}). Google's Compute Engine runs its virtual machines on KVM
\cite{Google_GCE_Overview}, and \acs{AWS} replaced its earlier hypervisor with
one based on KVM in its Nitro system \cite{AWS_Nitro_Journey,AWS_EC2_FAQ}.

These foundations matter for the benchmarking study in \cref{sec:method-study4}.
Every machine measured there is a virtual machine running on a hypervisor, and
much of the remaining difference between providers comes not from the hypervisor
but from the hardware beneath it and the way storage and networks are attached.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

This thesis draws on three bodies of earlier work, each of which covers part of
the question it asks.

The first concerns how to compare cloud providers fairly. Gillam and colleagues
measured four infrastructure clouds, from Amazon, Rackspace and IBM, alongside a
private OpenStack installation, using a set of small, focused benchmarks
\cite{Gillam_FairBenchmarking_2013}. They found that performance varies not only
between providers but also, sometimes substantially, between virtual machines of
apparently the same specification from the same provider, and they argued that
pricing based on the performance actually delivered would be fairer than fixed
prices. Later work on cloud benchmarking, reviewed in \cref{sec:method-study4},
built on these concerns. This thesis follows Gillam and colleagues in setting each
provider against the best result achieved, and applies the approach to European
providers that the earlier studies did not cover.

The second concerns European cloud sovereignty as a political question. Blancato
argues that the European Union uses rules and industrial policy gathered under the
name of data sovereignty for two purposes at once: to protect the confidentiality
of European data, and to counter the dominance of American providers in the
European cloud market \cite{Blancato_CloudSovereigntyNexus_2024}. He develops this
through two cases, Gaia-X and the European Alliance for Industrial Data, Edge and
Cloud. His study explains why the Union cares about sovereignty, but it works at
the level of policy and does not assess individual providers.

The third is the European Commission's own Cloud Sovereignty Framework
\cite{EC_DIGIT_CloudSovereignty_2025}, which turns sovereignty into eight
objectives and five assurance levels that can be applied to a provider. It is
written for public procurement and leaves it to the buyer to apply it, and it
does not report how existing providers score.

What this thesis adds is the connection between the three. It applies the
Commission's framework to eighteen European providers, question by question and
with every answer tied to a published source. It sets the result beside what the
same providers can actually offer an enterprise with real requirements, beside
the money behind them, and beside the measured speed of their machines. Where
Blancato explains why sovereignty matters to the Union and Gillam and colleagues
show how to compare clouds fairly, this thesis asks which European providers can
deliver sovereignty in practice, and at what cost in services, money and
performance, for one enterprise moving away from \acs{AWS}.
